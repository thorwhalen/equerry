# Positioning page, publishing rhythm, voice

A fill-in page per offer or product, after April Dunford's method [45]. The filled pages are instance material (they name offers, customers and competitors) and live in `<Teams>/marketing/references/`, never here. Last checked 2026-11-09.

## The positioning page

Fill the components in this order: each one depends on the one before. Every answer carries its evidence; an answer without evidence is marked **to verify** and becomes a question for the principal or for customer interviews.

```markdown
# Positioning: <offer or product>          updated <date>   next review <date>

## 1. Competitive alternatives
What would the best-fit customers do if this did not exist? (Another product, a consultant, an in-house hire, a spreadsheet, nothing.) Evidence: <what customers said they used before>.

## 2. Unique attributes
What does this have that those alternatives lack? Capabilities, method, expertise, delivery. Evidence: <a demonstration, a result, a feature, a credential>.

## 3. Value (and proof)
What do those attributes enable for the customer? One value per attribute cluster, in the customer's terms, with its proof. Evidence: <a measured outcome, a quote with permission>.

## 4. Best-fit customers
Who cares most about that value? Observable characteristics (situation, size, role, trigger), not demographics. Evidence: <who bought, who stayed, who referred>.

## 5. Market category
The frame that makes the value obvious to those customers. An existing category unless there is a reason to create one. Evidence: <how best-fit customers describe what they are buying>.

## Optional: relevant trends
A trend that makes this matter now for these customers, only if it is real and evidenced.

## The one sentence
For <best-fit customers> who <situation>, <offer> is a <category> that <value>, unlike <main alternative>, because <unique attribute>.

## Open questions (at most two for the principal)
```

Checks before the page is used: every component has evidence or a "to verify"; the sentence uses only what the components support; the category is one a customer would search for; the page has been read by legal if it names a competitor.

## The publishing rhythm

The rule: small and steady beats ambitious and abandoned. A rhythm the principal does not keep is worse than none, because it trains the audience to expect nothing.

- **Reuse first.** Before asking for new work, list what the principal already produces that can be published with light editing: release notes of public libraries, READMEs (a library's README is its landing page), talks and their slides, training material, research reports written for the principal's own decisions (only what is public by the placement test), answers already written in issues and forums.
- **One anchor, a few derivatives.** One substantial piece per cycle (default: one a month: a talk, an essay, a tutorial, a release with a story), and a few short derivatives from it on the channels the best-fit customers actually read.
- **Weekly plan, monthly review.** The weekly plan is a working note; the monthly review reads the scorecard and keeps, changes or drops a channel.
- **Fit the time budget.** The rhythm is sized to the principal's stated hours for marketing; the team says what to drop when it does not fit.
- **Nothing is published by the team.** Every piece is a draft for the principal, delivered with annals or the instance's desk.

## The voice

The principal's reputation is the asset, so every outgoing text sounds like the principal and carries no machine-writing tells.

1. **Known reader** (an email, a reply, a pitch to a person): acquaint-write, which reads that person's record and applies deslop.
2. **Every draft**: deslop, at the reader's tolerance of machine-sounding text.
3. **Check**: ductus (`ductus gauge draft.md`, or the `ductus-gauge` skill for a full reading with a judgment pass). Rewrite what leans strongly machine-written; ductus describes text, it never accuses a person, and its lean is evidence, not a percentage.
4. **Never invent specifics** (a number, a customer, an anecdote) to make a text sound real; a missing specific is a question for the principal.

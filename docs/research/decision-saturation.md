# Decision saturation: when the human approves blindly, and how to design against it

Theme 3 of the principal-model research. Written 2026-11-18 for equerry (many agents, one principal, dozens of sessions across several projects). It does not repeat the alert-override and automation-bias citations already in `principal-model-and-review-organisation.md` or `briefs-and-attention-triage.md` (override of 49-96% of drug alerts, the Ancker repeat-alert result, the 70% reliability rule of thumb, Horvitz bounded deferral); it extends them. Claims I could only check at abstract or secondary level are marked "unverified"; estimates and my own models are labelled as such.

## 1. Verdict

1. **High acceptance is not the same as rubber-stamping, and the literature gives no universal threshold.** If agents are mostly right, a 93% approval rate is the correct behaviour. Anthropic reports that users accept 93% of manual Claude Code permission prompts and names the result "approval fatigue" [1], but acceptance alone cannot separate "the prompts were fine" from "nobody was reading". The only direct way to tell them apart is **performance on items known to be wrong** (canaries, section 3), plus a few behavioural proxies (time, reading depth, drift). Equerry's current metrics (false auto-approve rate, items shown per day, override rate) measure the system's output; none measures the human's detection ability. Add one.
2. **Ground the design in vigilance, repetition and learned inattention, not in "decision fatigue".** The depletion account is the weakest-evidenced piece of the theme: the best-known field result (hungry judges) has a plausible artifact explanation [2][3], and the preregistered multisite ego-depletion test found an effect indistinguishable from zero [4]. By contrast, effortful-attention decrement in monitoring [5], automation complacency and bias [6], and repetition-driven alert override [7] are replicated and directly relevant. The practical implication is the same (fewer, newer, more varied items) but the justification differs, and so do the right metrics: count repeats and run length, not "decisions made today".
3. **A gate that the human does not genuinely exercise can be worse than no gate.** A 2024 meta-analysis of 106 experiments found that human-AI combinations did on average worse than the better of human or AI alone, and worst on decision tasks [8]. Oversight has a cost (attention, latency, false assurance) and it only pays when the human adds information the system lacks. The design question is therefore not "how do we make the principal review more carefully" but "which decisions does the principal have private information or authority on, and which are we merely asking them to sign".
4. **Decide what reaches the human with an explicit value-of-asking rule, calibrate it on canary performance, and widen autonomy only from measured evidence.** The decision-theoretic base is old and well founded (Horvitz's expected utility of asking versus acting [9], selective prediction [10], learning to defer [11]); the new element for equerry is that the attention cost is **not constant**: it rises with volume and repetition, so the marginal item is more expensive than the first.

## 2. What the research says

### 2.1 Automation bias, complacency, and what actually predicts them

- **Definitions.** Parasuraman and Manzey's integrative review treats complacency (insufficient monitoring and verification of an automated function) and automation bias (over-use of automated recommendations, producing omission errors when the aid misses something and commission errors when it advises wrongly) as overlapping manifestations of attention allocation. They appear in novices and experts, occur mainly under multitasking load, and are not removed by practice or by instructions alone (abstract-level source) [6]. This describes the principal's situation exactly: dozens of concurrent sessions is heavy multitasking.
- **Commission versus omission** is the useful split for equerry. A *recommended* item that is wrong invites commission errors (approve a bad suggestion); a problem the agents never surfaced is an omission error. Skitka and colleagues found both in a simulated flight task, and that participants held accountable for their overall performance verified more [12] (cited from memory; the existing agent-ops report cites the related NASA report). Accountability that is real, not ceremonial, is a lever.
- **Experience of failure helps, selectively.** A Manzey-group study found that experiencing automation misses reduced complacency toward the alarm function and omission errors, but did not change reliance on the aid's diagnostic advice or commission errors (as summarised in a search abstract; the full text was not read; unverified) [13]. Implication: exposing the principal to some seeded failures of the *alarm* layer (what was not surfaced) is more likely to help than hoping seeded bad recommendations alone retrain judgement.
- **Human-AI teams are not automatically better.** The 370-result meta-analysis [8] found negative average synergy, with large heterogeneity: decision tasks fared worst, creation tasks better, and gains were more likely when the human was the stronger party alone. Caution: the studies are mostly lab tasks with single decisions, not the delegation of many mixed-stakes actions; and press summaries overstate the result as "worse than either" in every case [8].
- **Calibrated reliance as the target.** Lee and See frame the goal as trust calibrated to the automation's actual reliability, with misuse and disuse as the two failure modes [14] (cited in the agent-ops report; not re-fetched here).

### 2.2 Vigilance decrement

The vigilance decrement (detection falls over a watch) goes back to Mackworth's radar-clock experiments [15] (from memory; unverified here). The modern reading is that monitoring is effortful, not mindless: converging behavioural, neural and subjective evidence shows vigilance is hard mental work and stressful, and the review explicitly addresses human monitoring of automated subsystems [5]. Applied evidence from baggage screening shows the same pattern is task-load dependent: in one threat-image-projection dataset, hit rate fell with time on task only when the image rate was high [16] (secondary summary; unverified at paper level). The takeaway for equerry is that reviewing agent output is a vigilance task whose decrement worsens with **rate and run length**, and that the decrement can be arrested by breaks and by rare-event design (seeded items), which is what screening does.

### 2.3 Alert fatigue and override

The earlier reports already cite the 49-96% override range and the Ancker result. One additional point worth carrying forward: in Ancker et al., acceptance was associated with repeated alerts and work complexity but not with overall workload or time since deployment (abstract-level; the "30% per extra reminder" figure comes from a repository abstract and was not confirmed in the full text) [7]. That makes **repetition** and **per-encounter count** the two controllable levers, which fits a per-day budget plus a dedupe key.

Industrial alarm management supplies the one family of numeric operating benchmarks for human alarm load. EEMUA 191 guidance (a guideline, not a mandatory standard) is: steady-state average under one alarm per 10 minutes, under 10 in the first 10 minutes of a major upset; tiered descriptions run from "very likely acceptable" (under 1 per 10 min) to "very likely unacceptable" (over 10 per 10 min); a Honeywell-led consortium survey found only about a third of consoles met the steady-state guideline and 2 of 37 came close in upsets [17]. These are for a trained operator watching one plant, so treat them as an order-of-magnitude anchor, not a target. Rough translation (my estimate): over an 8-hour attentive day, "very likely acceptable" is about 50 alarms; the EEMUA daily framings quoted by secondary sources (about 150 "acceptable", about 300 "manageable") assume continuous console work [17].

### 2.4 Decision fatigue: the replication controversy

- **The famous result.** Danziger, Levav and Avnaim-Pesso reported parole approvals falling within sessions from about 65% to near 0 and resetting after breaks, attributed to mental depletion [18].
- **Critiques.** Weinshall-Margel and Shapard noted that the boards are panels, not single judges, and that case order is not random (a prisoner with a lawyer was more likely to be scheduled right after a break) [3]. Glöckner showed by simulation that an artifact alone can generate a similar pattern: favourable rulings take longer, and boards that plan minimally avoid starting a long case just before a break [2]. Danziger and colleagues replied that the effect persists under further tests [18b], so the dispute is not formally closed, but the magnitude is widely considered overestimated.
- **The mechanism it was taken to support.** A 23-lab registered replication of the ego-depletion effect reported d = 0.04, and a 36-lab, N = 3,531 preregistered multisite paradigmatic test reported a confirmatory d = 0.06, not significant, with exploratory analyses giving d = 0.08 and Bayesian results about equivocal [4]. The 23-lab figure is as quoted in Glöckner [2] (unverified at source).
- **What survives.** Decision quality and speed may still change with time and load, but the specific claim "a finite stock of self-control is spent by each decision" lacks reliable support. For design, this argues against a "daily willpower budget" and for budgets framed on **volume, repetition, and run length of similar items**, which is where replicated effects sit.

### 2.4b Consent fatigue and click-through in security prompts

- A large field study of Chrome and Firefox found that 9% to 23% of malware and phishing warnings were clicked through, versus about 33% of Firefox and about 70% of Chrome SSL warnings; the authors attribute much of the difference to design (one click to bypass in Chrome versus three steps in Firefox) (secondary and abstract summaries; not read in full) [19]. Two lessons: click-through is a property of the interface as much as of the user, and warnings that are usually false alarms train bypass.
- Anthropic's own account of Claude Code permissions is the closest direct analogue: 93% of manual prompts are accepted and constant prompts cause people to stop paying close attention [1]. Their response is a classifier that decides which actions need the user. Its disclosed error rates are instructive: the full pipeline has a 0.4% false-positive rate on real traffic and a 17% false-negative rate on real "overeager" actions (n = 52, so wide uncertainty), which the authors call "the honest number"; after 3 consecutive or 20 total denials the system stops and escalates to the human [1]. A later claim of a 97% approval rate appears in secondary coverage only and I could not confirm it against a primary source (unverified) [1b].
- Anthropic's autonomy-measurement study reports that experienced users auto-approve more (about 20% of sessions for under 50 sessions, over 40% by about 750) but also interrupt more often (secondary summary of the primary page; verify exact numbers before quoting) [20]. This is a useful existence proof that oversight can **shift from approving each step to monitoring and intervening**, and that "more auto-approve" is not itself evidence of decay.

### 2.5 Code review as the nearest equerry-like precedent

The strongest industrial dataset (SmartBear/Cisco, about 2,500 reviews, 3.2M lines) found defect detection fell once reviews exceeded roughly 200-400 lines or 60-90 minutes; the exact cutoffs differ between summaries, it is one 2006 dataset, and the "attention budget" explanation is commentary rather than a tested mechanism [21]. Rubber-stamping ("LGTM") is described in the practitioner literature and a 2024 paper on "LGTM smells" (abstract only seen); I found no direct empirical estimate of rubber-stamp rates by size [21]. Treat "defect yield drops with size and duration" as plausible but thin evidence.

### 2.6 Responsibility without control

- **Moral crumple zone.** Elish: in automated systems, the human nearest the system can absorb blame and consequences for failures they had little power to prevent, and the system's design is protected at their expense (cases: Three Mile Island, Air France 447) [22]. If equerry's principal is the approver of record for 40 sessions' output, the risk is not only error but being the designated fall guy. Design response: record what the principal saw and what the system withheld (the tracing record), and do not present volume that cannot be reviewed as consent.
- **Meaningful human control.** Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven require a **tracking** condition (the system responds to the relevant reasons of the humans and to the facts of the environment) and a **tracing** condition (some responsible human can be identified in the design or use history who has the capacity to understand and exert control) [23]. A principal who cannot practically review what is shown them fails tracing even if they click approve. This turns "decision saturation" from a usability problem into a legitimacy test: an approval is meaningful only if the person could have said no, in time, with adequate information.

## 3. How blind approval is measured

No standard instrument exists for "rubber-stamping" in agent oversight (I found none; this is a gap). Combine the following. All thresholds below are **estimates for pilot calibration, not published standards**, unless cited.

| Signal | How computed | Why it works | Threshold in practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Canary detection rate** (seeded known-bad items) | share of seeded defective or out-of-policy items the principal rejects, edits or escalates | the only direct measure of detection; analogue of threat-image projection in baggage screening, where TIP gives per-screener hit rates and about 100 events per person are suggested for minimum reliability of about 0.7 [16] | published reliability needs about 100 events; for equerry compute over a rolling window and track the trend, not a pass mark; a fall below the principal's own baseline is the alarm |
| **Time to decide, relative to the item's own reading time** | decision latency divided by an estimate of reading time (words/rate) for the shown payload | very short latencies on long items imply no reading; practitioner anti-pattern lists use "very short latency, fast approvals" as the rubber-stamp marker [21] | flag items decided in under a fraction of estimated reading time (start with 25%, my estimate) |
| **Acceptance drift** | acceptance rate for a stable item class, week over week, with agent accuracy held fixed | rising acceptance while measured agent accuracy is flat suggests declining scrutiny | alert on a sustained upward trend beyond sampling noise; no published number |
| **Consistency on repeated items** | re-show a near-duplicate of a past item (same facts, new wording) and compare the answer | measures reliability of the principal's own judgement; the same test-retest logic as the prior report's [1] consistency ceiling | disagreement with own earlier decision above the baseline set in a calibration week |
| **Reading depth / engagement** | expansions opened, scroll depth, edits made, typed reasons, "why" tapped | rubber-stamping looks like decide-without-open | share of decisions with any engagement action; falling share is the signal |
| **Interaction-pattern entropy and streaks** | run length of identical answers; uniformity of timing | long runs of "approve" at constant cadence are what a habit looks like | long-run flags (for example, 15 in a row, estimate) |
| **Override and edit rate** | share of recommendations changed | too low can be blind approval; too high means the recommender is poor | use the existing dashboard metric, but read it with canaries |
| **Post-hoc outcome audit** | sample approved items; check later whether they caused rework or incident | measures whether approvals were good, not only whether attentive | sample-based, monthly |

Caveats. Time and engagement proxies can be gamed by a tired but diligent person and by a fast but expert one. Seeded items must be realistic: in screening, about a quarter of TIP images were rated as having artifacts and a quarter as unrealistic, and familiarity with a small library of images inflates detection [16], so rotate and generate canaries from real past mistakes rather than a fixed set. Canaries have an ethical dimension (testing the principal): the principal must consent and the results must be theirs, not a management score.

## 4. Countermeasures, with evidence and cost

| Countermeasure | Evidence | Cost and limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive forcing functions** (make the person think before the recommendation: wait, commit a guess, or an on-demand request) | In a 199-participant study, three forcing designs reduced overreliance on AI recommendations relative to simple explanations, but did not remove it; participants liked the effective designs least; benefit was greatest for people high in Need for Cognition [24] | friction, dislike, unequal benefit; do not apply to everything |
| **Show the recommendation after the human's own judgement** | In Gajos and Mamykina, decide-first-then-see-AI improved decisions but did not produce learning; explanation-only without a recommendation produced both better decisions and learning [25] | doubles the work per item; reserve for high-stakes decisions |
| **Evaluative AI** (evidence for and against hypotheses the human holds, not a verdict) | A position paper, with a framework and a call for empirical test; argues that recommendations take control from the human and invite blind following or ignoring [26] | proposed, not demonstrated at scale; unverified benefit |
| **Make explanations cost-effective** | Overreliance falls only when the task is hard, the explanation is easy to use, and engaging has benefit; with easy tasks or hard explanations explanations did nothing (5 studies, N = 731, maze tasks) [27] | suggests short, simple "why" and rewarding verification, not long rationales |
| **Friction proportional to stakes** | Chrome versus Firefox SSL bypass rates (about 70% versus 33%) illustrate that friction changes behaviour [19]; Anthropic's design escalates only after repeated denials and gates by action risk [1] | do not make low-risk actions costly; keep friction for irreversible or costly ones |
| **Seeded/canary items** | Screening practice with feedback after each seeded threat [16] | needs realism, rotation, and consent |
| **Varying presentation** | habituation to a repeated warning is the mechanism behind the repeat-alert effect [7] (the earlier brief report also cites attention decay over days) | cheap; vary layout and ordering of the same decision slots; do not vary the meaning |
| **Batching and a per-day item budget** | EEMUA alarm-rate benchmarks [17]; Google SRE rule that pages must be urgent, actionable and novel (cited in the prior report) | the budget is a design constraint, not a proven optimum; the right number is a pilot outcome |
| **Experience of failure** | experiencing misses reduced complacency toward the alarm function but not toward diagnostic advice [13] (unverified) | shows seeding the alarm layer, not only the advice layer |
| **Real accountability** | Skitka et al. [12] (from memory) | in a one-principal organisation, accountability is automatic; the risk is the moral crumple zone [22] |

Note that no source I found tests any of these in a setting like equerry's (many parallel agents, mixed stakes, one expert principal). The evidence is lab or clinical; extrapolation is an estimate.

## 5. Deciding what reaches the human

### 5.1 Expected value of asking

Horvitz's mixed-initiative principles divide the decision space into autonomous action, dialogue (ask), and inaction by two probability thresholds derived from expected utility; the alert work computes an expected cost of interruption by weighting the cost of each user state by its likelihood [9]. A one-line equerry version (my model, not from the sources): ask when

`P(principal would change the decision) x loss avoided if changed  >  attention cost(c_ask)  +  decay cost to future attention`

where the last term is what the saturation literature adds: each shown item **lowers the value of the next** through habituation and repetition [7]. If `c_ask` is treated as constant, the rule over-asks. Treat the daily budget as a shadow price: the threshold rises until the expected number of shown items matches the budget, and the ranking picks the items with the highest expected value per unit of attention.

Practical note: the quantity `P(would change)` is the same disagreement-risk estimate the principal-model report proposes; this makes the two reports one system. Calibrate it on the principal's later overrides, and on canary results, since an over-confident model that assumes "would approve" removes exactly the items where oversight is valuable.

### 5.2 Selective prediction and learning to defer

- **Selective classification** gives a model a reject option with a guaranteed risk at a chosen coverage; Geifman and El-Yaniv's softmax-response threshold achieved 2% top-5 ImageNet error with probability 99.9% at about 60% coverage [10]. The risk-coverage curve is the right object: equerry's "auto-approve" is coverage, and "false auto-approve rate" is risk.
- **Learning to defer** trains the model together with a rejector that sends cases to the human where the human is better, rather than where the model is unsure; the first formulation (Madras et al.) was not statistically consistent, and Mozannar and Sontag proposed a consistent convex surrogate trained from the expert's decisions [11]. For equerry: the rejector should send items on which *the principal* has an advantage (private context, taste, authority), not merely low-confidence items.
- **Active learning** asks the human the questions that teach the model most; the standard survey is Settles [28] (from memory; unverified here). Caveat for equerry: informative items and important items are different. Uncertainty sampling picks borderline cases, which are often low-stakes; combine with stakes so that learning questions are rationed and never pushed into the high-stakes queue.
- **Levels of autonomy.** Feng, McDonald and Zhang define five levels by the user's role: operator, collaborator, consultant, approver, observer, and treat autonomy as a design choice separable from capability, with "autonomy certificates" as a governance idea (level names verified; certificate detail secondary) [29]. Sheridan and Verplank's ten-level scale and Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens' four-stage model (acquisition, analysis, decision, action) [30] (from memory; unverified here) are the classic references, and the stage view matters: automate information acquisition and analysis more freely than selection and action.

### 5.3 Widening autonomy safely

1. **Per decision class, not globally.** Move each class (for example "merge a docs-only PR") through observer-shadow, ask, approve-with-default, auto-with-audit. Anthropic's own path is a classifier gate per action plus automatic fallback to asking after repeated blocks [1].
2. **Promotion needs evidence**: a minimum number of principal decisions on that class, an override rate below a stated bound with a confidence interval (counting only items the principal demonstrably engaged with), and zero severe misses in the audit sample. Use the **honest number** discipline [1]: report the false-negative rate with its sample size, since n = 52 gave a 17% figure with wide uncertainty.
3. **Keep a sample of auto-handled items in the queue** (audit slice), which doubles as the canary stream and as the detector for drift in either the agent or the principal.
4. **Automatic demotion**: if the audit slice or a canary shows a severe miss, or the consecutive-denial counter trips, the class returns to asking [1].
5. **Reversibility first.** Only reversible, bounded-blast-radius classes are eligible for auto, consistent with the principal-model report.

## 6. Terminology and things not asked about

- **Standard terms.** Automation bias, automation complacency, errors of commission and omission, alert fatigue, alarm flood, cry-wolf effect (Breznitz, from memory), vigilance decrement, out-of-the-loop performance problem (Endsley and Kiris, from memory), ironies of automation (Bainbridge, [31] from memory), calibrated trust, overreliance and underreliance, appropriate reliance, human-in-the-loop versus on-the-loop, selective prediction (reject option), learning to defer, human-AI complementarity, rubber-stamping, approval or consent fatigue, moral crumple zone, meaningful human control (tracking and tracing), manage by exception.
- **Bainbridge's irony** is the deepest point missing from the brief: the more reliable the automation, the less practised and less vigilant the human, so the fewer errors they catch when it matters [31]. Seeded items and occasional manual handling are the standard remedies (skill maintenance).
- **Skill atrophy and learning.** Gajos and Mamykina show AI assistance can improve decisions without learning [25]. A principal who only approves may lose the model of the work that makes their rare overrides good. Track whether the principal still *originates* decisions (not only answers them).
- **Sycophantic recommenders and framing.** A recommendation phrased with confidence or a default pre-selected is itself a nudge; evaluative or neutral framing and non-default options reduce it (Miller's argument, [26]). Not tested for coding agents.
- **Agents learn what is approved.** If agents (or the recommender) are rewarded by approvals, they will shape items to be approvable; this is a Goodhart risk that approval-rate metrics encourage. Prefer outcome-based measures.
- **Adversarial or accidental false urgency.** An agent that always flags "needs you" is itself a noisy producer; the producer-level count and a producer reliability score (hit rate of its escalations) are standard alarm-rationalisation practice.
- **Pre-commitment.** A principal can set rules in a calm moment ("always escalate anything touching billing"), which removes decisions from the in-the-moment queue (policy-as-code); this is the main alternative to asking.

## 7. Five pilots for equerry (each about one week)

1. **Canary seeding and detection rate.** Insert 1-2 realistic known-bad items per day (derived from real past agent mistakes and from the principal's past overrides, rotated, with the principal's prior consent and a quiet reveal after the decision). Success: you get a per-week detection rate with at least 8-10 canaries, a baseline for the principal, and at least one real finding about which item types slip through. A detection rate far below the principal's stated expectation means the oversight is partly theatre for that class; the response is to reduce volume or change presentation for that class, not to scold. If the rate is near 100% on easy canaries, add harder ones.
2. **Decide-first for the top-stakes slice.** For the highest-stakes 2-3 items a day, show the facts and options first, collect the principal's tentative choice with one tap, then reveal the recommendation (Gajos and Mamykina [25], Bucinca [24]). Compare, over the week, agreement with the recommendation, the share of changes after reveal, and subjective effort. Success: a measurable share of cases where the principal's first answer differs from the recommendation (so the recommendation is not being rubber-stamped) at acceptable added time; failure: no differences and clear time cost (then restrict to canary items only).
3. **Daily budget with a shadow price and a visible overflow queue.** Fix a cap (start at 12 pushed decisions a day, an estimate to tune), rank items by expected value per unit of attention, move the overflow to an audit digest instead of the live queue, and dedupe repeats by key (Ancker [7]). Success: median decision time on shown items rises or holds (more reading, not less), repeat share falls, the principal reports no important item found only in the overflow, and the override rate on shown items goes up (a sign that what is shown is more consequential). Failure: a miss found in overflow, which tells you which class the ranker undervalues.
4. **Engagement telemetry and drift dashboard (no UI change).** Log decision latency against estimated reading time, expansions opened, run length, and acceptance by item class; add a repeat-consistency probe (one re-shown near-duplicate every few days). Success: after a week, the logs show whether blind-approval patterns exist and for which class; set thresholds from the principal's own distribution (for example, the bottom 10% of latency per item length), not from published numbers. Cheapest pilot; do it first.
5. **One decision class promoted to auto-with-audit, with automatic demotion.** Pick one reversible, high-volume, low-stakes class (for example, routine dependency bumps that pass CI). Auto-handle it, keep a 10-20% audit slice in the digest, and use the Anthropic-style fallback rule of demotion after 3 consecutive principal rejections [1]. Success: at least 30 audited items, no severe miss, principal rejections near zero, and visible reduction in shown items per day; report the false-negative rate with its sample size, not a bare percentage.

Order of play: 4 first (no risk, gives baselines), then 1 and 3 together, then 5, then 2.

## 8. What could not be verified, and open gaps

- The Anthropic 93% figure appears on Anthropic's own pages [1]; the later 97% figure and the autonomy-study percentages (about 20% to over 40% full auto-approve) are from secondary coverage; verify before quoting [1b][20].
- Parasuraman and Manzey, Bahner et al., Vaccaro et al., Gajos and Mamykina, Bucinca et al., Vasconcelos et al., Miller, Elish, Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven, Geifman and El-Yaniv, Madras and Mozannar, Horvitz, Feng et al. were confirmed at abstract or listing level through search, not read in full text. Numeric claims from them are as stated in those abstracts.
- Mackworth, Skitka 1999, Settles, Sheridan and Verplank, Parasuraman-Sheridan-Wickens, Bainbridge, Breznitz, Endsley and Kiris were cited from memory and not re-fetched; DOIs given are best-recollection and should be checked.
- No source tests cognitive forcing, canary seeding, or budgets in a multi-agent coding-oversight setting, and no published threshold for "too fast to be reading" or for a safe daily item count in knowledge work exists that I could find; all such numbers above are estimates to be calibrated by the pilots.
- Not covered: group or two-person review as a countermeasure (second reviewers also show social loafing), regulatory framings (EU AI Act human oversight provisions), and sleep or circadian effects on vigilance.
